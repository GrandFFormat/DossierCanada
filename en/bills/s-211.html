What it does, in plain language
  • Requires the Minister of Canadian Heritage to develop a national framework governing sports betting advertising in Canada.
  • Calls for measures to restrict such advertising, including limits on the number, scope or location of ads, and limits or a ban on celebrities and athletes promoting sports betting.
  • Requires national standards for preventing and identifying harmful gambling and addiction, and for supporting people affected by it.
  • Mandates consultations with several federal ministers, provinces and territories, provincial gaming regulators, medical and research communities, the advertising and gambling industries, and Indigenous communities and organizations.
  • Sets a one-year deadline after the Act comes into force to table a report in Parliament containing the framework and an implementation strategy, to be posted on the department's website within 10 days of tabling.
  • Requires a follow-up report within five years of the first one, covering which measures were implemented, how effective they were, and why others were not.
  • Directs the CRTC to review its own regulations and policies for their effectiveness in reducing harms from widespread sports betting advertising, reporting to the Minister no later than one year after royal assent.

✋ What does “challenging” mean?

Clicking “Ask for an explanation” is a citizen request for the bill's sponsor to explain it in plain language — and for us to keep an eye on it together.

An account (email) is required — one request per person, no anonymous requests. 🔥 At 1,000 requests on the same bill, a public post is made, in the hope an MP agrees to sponsor a petition to the House.

It works on any bill, including one that has already received royal assent — that is often the one people want to understand.

How to read a vote

Recorded vote

Members vote one by one and each choice is entered in the public record. That is what lets us say exactly who voted what — no interpretation.

Passed is not law

A passed vote clears ONE stage. A bill must pass both chambers and then receive royal assent before it becomes law.

Paired is not absent

In the Commons, two members from opposing sides can agree not to vote: their voices cancel out. It is neither an absence nor an abstention.

Promesses électorales

What was promised in 2025, and what Parliament did with it. No verdict.

Why no verdict?

Because a measure written into an assented law is not necessarily applied, or funded as promised — and because calling it “kept” or “broken” on your behalf, using only what a monitoring site can read, would be dishonest.

So you get both sides, each sourced: on the left, the promise quoted word for word from the party's own platform, with a link to the document. On the right, the bills that implement it, with the sentence from the official summary that proves it. The judgment is yours.

DossierCanada › The rules

The site's rules

Where what DossierCanada publishes comes from, and what it will not do
← Back to overview

DossierCanada relies only on what an institution or a party has itself published, with a link to the original. And it says clearly what is missing.

  1. 1No invented data

    This is rule number one. If a piece of information is missing, or cannot be matched with certainty, the site says so (“not available”) instead of guessing. We do not fill in anything.

  2. 2Primary sources only

    We quote the institution or the party itself, never someone talking about it: LEGISinfo (Parliament of Canada) for bills; the House of Commons for MPs, votes, petitions and the sitting calendar; the Senate for senators and their votes; the Prime Minister's website for the Cabinet; the Office of the Commissioner of Lobbying for declared lobbying; the platform published by the party for promises. One exception, which is a service and not a fact: finding your MP by postal code goes through Represent (OpenNorth), which links a postal code to a riding.

  3. 3No news media

    Nothing is taken from the press. Journalists' work is not ours to reuse, and information reported second-hand cannot be checked against a document. A promise made at a press conference but absent from the platform published by the party therefore does not appear on the site.

  4. 4We never work around a protection

    We never work around a CAPTCHA or an anti-bot protection. Our tools always identify themselves by name, DossierCanada, never as a browser. The House of Commons protects the bulk download of its petitions with a reCAPTCHA: we do not use it, we only read the public list its website shows to everyone. The file from the Office of the Commissioner of Lobbying was closed to automated tools: we asked for permission, and the Office authorized our tool in September 2026.

  5. 5Promises are quoted word for word

    Every promise is an exact quote from the platform published by the party, in its own language: never translated, summarized or reworded by us. A script checks each quote back against the source page, and a quote that cannot be found word for word is not published. The sentence linking a promise to a bill is also copied from that bill's official summary. We do not use artificial intelligence to “find” promises: it invents them. Every card carries a “See the source” link.

  6. 6No verdict

    The site never says “promise kept” or “promise broken”, and it does not rate or judge anyone. It places the promise and the bills side by side, each with its source. The lobbying shown is what is declared in the official registry, without comment. You are the judge.

  7. 7The official text always prevails

    Plain-language summaries are generated by artificial intelligence from the official text of the bill as introduced, and are labelled as such: they may not reflect amendments adopted since. The link to the full text stays on every bill. If anything differs, the original document is the one that counts. The site is independent and has no official status.

  8. 8We explain what is missing

    When data is absent or incomplete, the page says so. A promise that leads to no bill is shown as such. Opposition parties are absent from the promises page, and the page says why: they do not control the legislative agenda. An absence on the site must never be read as silence from the institution or the party.

These rules apply everywhere on the site. See them at work on the promises page, and follow what changes in the site updates.

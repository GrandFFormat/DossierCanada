S-210 An Act respecting Ukrainian Heritage Month Assented to ▾
What it does, in plain language
  • Creates a new law, the Ukrainian Heritage Month Act.
  • Makes September, every year and across Canada, "Ukrainian Heritage Month".
  • Notes that more than 1.3 million Ukrainian-Canadians live in the country today.
  • Points out that the first recorded arrival of Ukrainians in Canada happened in September, more than 125 years ago.
  • Recognizes what Ukrainian-Canadians bring to Canada's social, economic, political and cultural life.
  • Mentions contributions in art, language, food, religion and folklore.
  • Sets no new duties, creates no new body and spends no money: the month is purely symbolic.

S-237 An Act respecting a Cities and Municipalities Day ISG In progress ▾
What it does, in plain language
  • Creates a new law, the Cities and Municipalities Day Act.
  • Names October 31 "Cities and Municipalities Day" across Canada, every year.
  • Makes clear the day is not a legal holiday, so stores, schools and offices stay open as usual.
  • Points out that Canada's 3,500 municipalities deliver daily services like water, roads, garbage pickup and snow removal.
  • Highlights the role of cities on housing, climate change, infrastructure and fighting poverty.
  • Notes that municipalities rely heavily on property taxes, which limits what they can do.
  • Matches the date the United Nations chose in 2013 for World Cities Day.

S-253 An Act to amend the Income Tax Act (child care expense deduction indexation) In progress ▾
What it does, in plain language
  • Raises the maximum amount of child care costs a parent can deduct from taxable income.
  • Increases the limit from $8,000 to $12,855 per child under age 7, starting with the 2027 tax year.
  • Increases the limit from $5,000 to $7,529 for other eligible children, starting in 2027.
  • Increases the limit from $11,000 to $16,345 for a child who qualifies for the disability tax credit.
  • Keeps today's amounts ($11,000, $8,000 and $5,000) for all tax years before 2027.
  • Ties the new amounts to the Consumer Price Index, a measure of the cost of living, for years after 2027.
  • Uses the 2027 amounts as the starting point for figuring out future yearly increases.
  • Gives the law the short name "Making Child Care More Affordable for Canadian Families Act."

C-34 An Act to enact the Digital Safety Act and the Digital Safety Commission of Canada Act and to make consequential amendments to other Acts 32 LPC In progress ▾
What it does, in plain language
  • Creates two new laws to set rules for online safety, with a focus on protecting children.
  • Applies to companies running social media services, chatbot services and other online services covered by the law.
Part 1new Digital Safety Act
  • Requires social media services to lower the risk that users see harmful content, to offer tools to block users and flag content, and to label computer-generated content.
  • Requires chatbot services to lower the risk of sending harmful content and to step in during a crisis.
  • Sets a minimum age to view pornographic content and requires design features that protect children.
  • Requires social media services to block, in Canada, content that sexually victimizes a child and intimate images shared without consent.
  • Lets anyone in Canada file a complaint about that content, after first trying to resolve it with the company.
  • Requires companies to file a digital safety plan showing they are meeting their duties.
  • Lets the federal Cabinet add other online services to these rules, without a new vote in Parliament.
Part 2new Digital Safety Commission of Canada Act
  • Creates the Digital Safety Commission of Canada to oversee how the new rules are followed.
  • Gives the Commission power to order a company to make content inaccessible to people in Canada.
  • Lets anyone in Canada send the Commission comments on what covered companies are doing.
  • Updates other laws to reflect the creation of the Commission.

C-38 An Act to amend the Excise Tax Act (extension of the federal fuel excise tax relief) LPC Assented to ▾
What it does, in plain language
  • Extends the pause on the federal fuel excise tax, already in place, until January 31, 2027.
  • Applies to unleaded gasoline, leaded and unleaded aviation gasoline, diesel fuel and aviation fuel.
  • Sets the tax at half the normal rates from February 1, 2027 to March 31, 2027.
  • Cuts the 10-cents-per-litre rate to 5 cents during those two months.
  • Cuts the 11-cents-per-litre rate to 5.5 cents over the same period.
  • Cuts the 4-cents-per-litre rate to 2 cents over the same period.
  • Names the new law the Canadian Fuel Affordability Act.
  • Makes the extension retroactive, starting September 8, 2026.

C-222 An Act to amend the Employment Insurance Act and the Canada Labour Code (death of a child) LPC In progress ▾
What it does, in plain language
  • Lets a parent receiving employment insurance benefits for a newborn or a child being adopted keep those benefits if the child dies during the benefit period.
  • Treats the parent as still caring for the child until the benefit period would normally end.
  • Frees that parent from filing a new claim or reports to prove they still qualify for benefits.
  • Extends the same protection to self-employed people receiving these benefits.
  • Removes this protection if the child died because of a crime and the parent is convicted of that crime.
  • Keeps maternity leave under the Canada Labour Code, for jobs regulated by Ottawa (banks, airlines, telecoms, etc.), even if the newborn dies during the leave.
  • Keeps parental leave running to its normal end if the child dies during that leave.
  • Gives the new law the short title Relieving Grieving Parents of an Administrative Burden Act (Evan's Law).

C-241 An Act to establish a national strategy respecting flood and drought forecasting LPC In progress ▾
What it does, in plain language
  • Creates a new law requiring the Minister of the Environment to build a national strategy for forecasting floods and droughts.
  • Brings in the ministers of Agriculture, Infrastructure, Natural Resources and Public Safety to help develop the strategy.
  • Requires talks with provinces, municipalities, Indigenous governing bodies, universities, industry and insurance companies.
  • Calls for a look at the benefits of national coordination, new spending and new forecasting technology.
  • Calls for a review of the need for modelling that flags homes and infrastructure at risk of flooding.
  • Requires a proposal for a national water forecasting service shared between Ottawa and the provinces.
  • Sets a two-year deadline to table the strategy in Parliament, with online publication within 10 days.
  • Requires a second report on how well the strategy works, five years after the first one is tabled.

S-209 An Act to restrict young persons’ online access to pornographic material 2 PSG In progress ▾
What it does, in plain language
  • Creates a new law, the Protecting Young Persons from Exposure to Pornography Act, aimed at commercial porn websites.
  • Makes it a crime for a business to let anyone under 18 access pornographic material online for commercial purposes.
  • Sets fines of up to $250,000 for a first offence and up to $500,000 for later ones.
  • Shields a business from conviction if it uses an age-verification or age-estimation method approved by the government.
  • Allows a defence when the content serves science, medicine, education or the arts, and spares search or hosting services that only incidentally give access.
  • Lets a federal enforcement body send a notice to the offending business, which then has 20 days to fix the problem.
  • Allows that body to ask the Federal Court to order Internet providers to block access to the material in Canada if nothing is fixed.
  • Requires age checks to be run by an independent third party, to protect privacy and to delete personal information once the check is done.
  • Requires the responsible minister to table a yearly report to Parliament on notices issued and court applications made.
  • Takes effect one year after the bill receives royal assent.

C-41 An Act to amend the Criminal Code and An Act to amend the Criminal Code (medical assistance in dying) and to make related amendments to other Acts LPC In progress ▾
What it does, in plain language
  • Confirms that a person cannot get medical assistance in dying when mental illness is the only reason, and removes the end date on that exclusion.
  • Lets a doctor or nurse practitioner provide medical assistance in dying to someone who has lost the ability to consent, if they signed a request ahead of time.
  • Requires that the person was diagnosed, before signing, with a serious and incurable illness expected to take away their ability to decide.
  • Requires the person to describe in the request the specific signs of the illness that they expect would cause unbearable suffering.
  • Bars going ahead if the person shows refusal or resistance through words, sounds or gestures; if there is doubt, a wait of at least 30 days applies.
  • Requires a second written opinion from another doctor or nurse practitioner confirming the person meets every criterion.
  • Allows these advance requests only in provinces that have their own rules in force and an official place where the requests are stored.
  • Sets a penalty of up to five years in prison for a practitioner who knowingly ignores these safeguards.
  • States that no one is forced to provide this service, and that affected veterans are treated as having died from their illness.

C-40 An Act respecting production, procurement and investment in relation to national defence and national security LPC In progress ▾
What it does, in plain language
  • Aims to strengthen Canada's defence sector by changing how Ottawa buys, produces and funds military and security equipment.
  • Sets up a new federal corporation and widens the buying and investment powers of the Public Works minister.
Part 1new Canadian Corporation for Defence Investment Act
  • Creates the Canadian Corporation for Defence Investment, a Crown corporation handling defence production, purchasing and investment.
  • Has the corporation work on behalf of the Canadian Forces, the Department of National Defence, or allied governments.
  • Adjusts other federal laws to fit the new corporation.
Part 2Defence Production Act and Access to Information Act
  • Expands the Defence Production Act to also cover supplies, projects and services tied to national security.
  • Gives the Public Works minister the power to buy these supplies and services for federal departments and agencies, with some exceptions.
  • Lets the minister build up stockpiles for defence, national security (including economic security) and for allied countries.
  • Gives the minister new financial powers, including investing in defence and security sectors, and sets new purchasing rules.
Part 3review of the defence measures
  • Requires a review of how the new corporation law and the changes to the Defence Production Act are working.

✋ What does “challenging” mean?

Clicking “Ask for an explanation” is a citizen request for the bill's sponsor to explain it in plain language — and for us to keep an eye on it together.

An account (email) is required — one request per person, no anonymous requests. 🔥 At 1,000 requests on the same bill, a public post is made, in the hope an MP agrees to sponsor a petition to the House.

It works on any bill, including one that has already received royal assent — that is often the one people want to understand.

How to read a vote

Recorded vote

Members vote one by one and each choice is entered in the public record. That is what lets us say exactly who voted what — no interpretation.

Passed is not law

A passed vote clears ONE stage. A bill must pass both chambers and then receive royal assent before it becomes law.

Paired is not absent

In the Commons, two members from opposing sides can agree not to vote: their voices cancel out. It is neither an absence nor an abstention.

Why no verdict?

Because a measure written into an assented law is not necessarily applied, or funded as promised — and because calling it “kept” or “broken” on your behalf, using only what a monitoring site can read, would be dishonest.

So you get both sides, each sourced: on the left, the promise quoted word for word from the party's own platform, with a link to the document. On the right, the bills that implement it, with the sentence from the official summary that proves it. The judgment is yours.

DossierCanada › The rules

The site's rules

Where what DossierCanada publishes comes from, and what it will not do
← Back to overview

DossierCanada relies only on what an institution or a party has itself published, with a link to the original. And it says clearly what is missing.

  1. 1No invented data

    This is rule number one. If a piece of information is missing, or cannot be matched with certainty, the site says so (“not available”) instead of guessing. We do not fill in anything.

  2. 2Primary sources only

    We quote the institution or the party itself, never someone talking about it: LEGISinfo (Parliament of Canada) for bills; the House of Commons for MPs, votes, petitions and the sitting calendar; the Senate for senators and their votes; the Prime Minister's website for the Cabinet; the Office of the Commissioner of Lobbying for declared lobbying; the platform published by the party for promises. One exception, which is a service and not a fact: finding your MP by postal code goes through Represent (OpenNorth), which links a postal code to a riding.

  3. 3No news media

    Nothing is taken from the press. Journalists' work is not ours to reuse, and information reported second-hand cannot be checked against a document. A promise made at a press conference but absent from the platform published by the party therefore does not appear on the site.

  4. 4We never work around a protection

    We never work around a CAPTCHA or an anti-bot protection. Our tools always identify themselves by name, DossierCanada, never as a browser. The House of Commons protects the bulk download of its petitions with a reCAPTCHA: we do not use it, we only read the public list its website shows to everyone. The file from the Office of the Commissioner of Lobbying was closed to automated tools: we asked for permission, and the Office authorized our tool in September 2026.

  5. 5Promises are quoted word for word

    Every promise is an exact quote from the platform published by the party, in its own language: never translated, summarized or reworded by us. A script checks each quote back against the source page, and a quote that cannot be found word for word is not published. The sentence linking a promise to a bill is also copied from that bill's official summary. We do not use artificial intelligence to “find” promises: it invents them. Every card carries a “See the source” link.

  6. 6No verdict

    The site never says “promise kept” or “promise broken”, and it does not rate or judge anyone. It places the promise and the bills side by side, each with its source. The lobbying shown is what is declared in the official registry, without comment. You are the judge.

  7. 7The official text always prevails

    Plain-language summaries are generated by artificial intelligence from the official text of the bill as introduced, and are labelled as such: they may not reflect amendments adopted since. The link to the full text stays on every bill. If anything differs, the original document is the one that counts. The site is independent and has no official status.

  8. 8We explain what is missing

    When data is absent or incomplete, the page says so. A promise that leads to no bill is shown as such. Opposition parties are absent from the promises page, and the page says why: they do not control the legislative agenda. An absence on the site must never be read as silence from the institution or the party.

These rules apply everywhere on the site. See them at work on the promises page, and follow what changes in the site updates.

Who defends your rights in court

Charities that challenge laws and government decisions in court. They do not hold the same positions — on purpose. DossierCanada is not affiliated with any of them and recommends none; they are listed alphabetically.
